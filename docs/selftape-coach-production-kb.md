# Self Tape Coach — Production Playbook (web-researched)

_Synthesized from 6 self-tape domains (lighting, framing, eyeline/reader, slate/delivery, sound, mistakes). 2026-06-14._

## Summary
A self-tape that gets watched is one the CD can instantly SEE and HEAR: a soft, evenly-lit, correctly-exposed face with live catchlit eyes, framed chest-up and horizontal at eye level, against a clean mid-tone backdrop, with crisp echo-free audio. Casting sorts fast — often 10-15 seconds, sometimes about 4 — so the opening frame, the eyes, and the first line of dialogue decide everything; a silhouette, a dark face, vertical video, or muddy sound is an instant skip no matter how good the acting is. The highest-leverage moves are the ones most actors neglect: buy a cheap mic, never backlight yourself, lock focus and exposure, put a real (or marked) eyeline beside the lens, and slate last so the performance lands first. Above all, follow the breakdown literally — orientation, file format, take count, slate placement, naming, deadline — because that's the part of the audition that proves you can be directed and trusted with the actual job.

## Setup Checklist (lighting + camera)
- Pick your space: a small room with soft surfaces (carpet, sofa, curtains, bed, hanging clothes). A walk-in closet is the classic echo-killer. Avoid empty rooms, hallways, and hard-floor/bare-wall spaces.
- Hang a clean, wrinkle-free solid backdrop in soft grey, blue, blue-grey, or green-grey (off-white/beige acceptable). Avoid black (floating-head), white-on-white, anything matching your shirt, and any clutter, art, doors, switches, or furniture in frame.
- Stand 3-4 feet (1m+) off the backdrop so your shadow falls on the floor, not the wall, and light can wrap.
- Set the camera on a tripod, landscape/horizontal (16:9), back camera, at EYE LEVEL (lens dead-level with your eyes, or a hair above — never below, never tilted up your nose).
- Key light: ONE big soft source (softbox or LED panel diffused through a sheet), ~1.5-2m from your face, slightly above eye level, angled down 30-45 degrees, off to one side — not dead-on, not overhead.
- Add fill on the opposite side (softer lamp, white foam-board bounce, or a window) to lift the shadow side. Aim for gentle modeling — not flat, not heavy contrast.
- Natural-light alternative: face a window with the CAMERA between you and the window so daylight lands on your face. NEVER sit with the window behind you (instant silhouette).
- Match color temperature to ONE family: all daylight (~5600K) or all tungsten (~3200K). Don't mix a daylight panel with a warm lamp and a window. Set white balance to match; warm/neutral beats clinical blue on skin.
- Confirm a catchlight — a small reflection of your source in each eye. If the eyes are dead/dark, raise or soften the key.
- Set up audio: external mic over the built-in. Lav clipped on the sternum ~6-8in below the chin, or a shotgun pointed at your mouth from 1-2ft just out of frame. No mic? Move yourself close to the phone.
- Hang a heavy blanket behind the camera to kill reflections; close all windows; turn off AC, fans, fridge, and any hum.
- Lock FOCUS and EXPOSURE (iPhone: tap-and-HOLD your face for AE/AF LOCK). Turn off auto gain (AGC); set audio levels manually. Set exposure so skin is bright with detail — not blown, not muddy.
- Set resolution to 1080p, 24 or 30fps (avoid 60fps soap-opera look). 720p is also a fine, lighter deliverable.
- Do a 5-second test take and play it back ON HEADPHONES: eyes lit, catchlight present, focus locked, skin tone natural, audio clean and echo-free. Fix before committing a full read.

## Framing & Eyeline
- Shot size: medium close-up, chest-up — top of frame just above your head with a small sliver of headroom, bottom edge from mid-chest to mid-bicep. Go a touch wider (mid-thigh) only for comedy or physical roles.
- Get close enough to read your face. Avoid both extremes: tiny figure in a wide empty room, or lens-in-your-nostrils tight.
- Eyes land on the upper-third gridline (rule of thirds). Frame to the left or right third, not dead center, to leave room for the eyeline.
- Camera at eye level, locked off on a tripod — zero movement, zero zoom.
- Eyeline just BESIDE the lens — roughly 6 inches off, within a 5-10 degree angle. Tight to the lens but NEVER full profile (the CD must see both eyes). Never down the barrel unless the breakdown says 'to camera.'
- Pick ONE side and commit (follow the 180 rule). Don't drift or switch sides between lines, or the scene's geography collapses.
- Put the reader physically next to the camera — almost straddling the tripod — at lens height, so your near-to-camera eyeline reads naturally.
- Plot multiple eyelines in advance: give each character/object/phone its own consistent point; put imagined images/memories on the OPPOSITE side from the reader.
- No reader on-set? Anchor your eyeline with a small sticker/tape mark beside the lens at eye level, or use a cue-aware self-reader app (Scene Reader, Rafy, ScenePartner, Act-On-Cue, Linus, Slatable); book a live human via WeAudition or Self Tape Reader for real chemistry.
- Stay centered on your mark and mostly still — the tight frame can't hold big movement.

## Slate & Delivery
- SLATE CONTENT: default is first and last (stage) name ONLY. Add height, agency, union status, and home base ONLY if the breakdown asks. Include only what's requested — over-slating (reciting credits/age/agency unprompted) reads as not following direction.
- SLATE ENERGY: deliver as a relaxed, warm version of yourself — 'half you, half the character.' Never slate in full character; the CD wants the real person first.
- SLATE PLACEMENT: for dramatic/TV/film, upload the slate as a SEPARATE file (the dominant Eco Cast/Actors Access norm). If forced to combine, put the slate at the END so the CD hits the performance first. Commercials are the exception — they usually want the slate at the HEAD.
- FULL-BODY SLATE: only when requested — deliver the name slate chest-up, then step back (or pan) to show full frame to just below the knees.
- SCENE FRAMING: chest-up MCU, landscape/horizontal. Reframe between slate and scene per instructions.
- FILE FORMAT: .mp4 (most universal) or .mov/.m4v, H.264. Convert AVCHD/raw camera files to mp4 first.
- RESOLUTION: 720p-1080p is plenty; don't chase 4K (bloats file size, uploads slow, no casting benefit).
- FILE SIZE: keep each file under 500MB (hard ceiling on Eco Cast), under 1GB total (Spotlight); aim under ~150MB per take when you can.
- FILE NAMING: clean and consistent — 'FirstName_LastName_Role' or 'Jane_Doe_Detective_Scene1.mp4'. Letters, numbers, underscores only. No spaces, slashes, emojis, or special characters (they break casting-system sorting/uploads).
- ONE clean continuous take per scene — no internal cuts, edits, music, or filters. Upload each scene/take/slate as its own clearly named file; reorder before submitting.
- TAKES: send at most two, and only if meaningfully different in choice. Never send two near-identical takes or three-plus.
- LENGTH: hit the requested length and pages only. Trim dead air at head and tail; don't pad with improvised pre-scene lines.
- Eco Cast LOCKS submissions after send — double-check order and naming before you hit submit. Read the breakdown twice; its instructions override every general rule. Submit early — review windows close hard.

## Sound
- Spend your first equipment dollar on AUDIO, not lighting or backdrop — it's the most undervalued element and the #1 technical pass/fail.
- Use an external mic, never the built-in: a clip-on lav (RODE smartLav+, Sennheiser), an on-camera shotgun (RODE VideoMic/Pro), or a USB condenser to a computer. A wired-headphone mic is the bare-minimum fallback.
- Lav placement: sternum/chest, ~6-8in (15cm) below the chin (thumb-to-pinky span from the mouth). Shotgun: aimed at your mouth from 1-2ft, just out of frame.
- No external mic? Move YOURSELF close to the phone — distance creates the hollow, echoey sound CDs hate.
- Record in a small soft-surfaced room; hang a heavy blanket behind the camera to kill reflections (cuts echo/outside noise ~70%). Avoid empty rooms, hallways, and hard floors.
- Kill every hum before rolling: AC, fans, extractor fan, fridge, plumbing. Close all windows; record during quiet hours if needed.
- Turn OFF auto gain control (AGC) and set levels manually — AGC pumps volume and surfaces hiss between lines.
- Record in the highest-quality/least-compressed setting, monitor on headphones while shooting, and ALWAYS play the take back on headphones before sending (the phone speaker hides hum, echo, and clipping).
- Reader volume: have them speak SOFTER than you and sit a few feet further back than the camera (phone mics grab the closest voice). Move the recording device closer to YOU, or clip a lav on yourself, to isolate your voice.
- A clean room beats software cleanup — avoid heavy noise-reduction that leaves the voice watery and artifact-y.

## Top Mistakes (cut in 5-10s)
- Backlit / sitting in front of a window so you go to silhouette — the single most-cited reason a tape gets cut.
- Bad audio: room echo, AC/traffic/pet hum, recording too far from the mic, or over-processed filters — the top TECHNICAL reason for rejection; if they strain to hear the first lines, they skip.
- Face too dark, washed-out, or blown out — the CD can't read your eyes or expression, which is the whole point.
- Vertical/portrait video — the fastest way to get skipped on a pro platform (tiny, sideways, black bars).
- Eyeline staring straight down the lens (when not directed) or so wide you're in profile — breaks the scene and hides the eyes.
- Reader louder than the actor, or one who over-acts/steals the scene — the CD can't hear or watch the right person.
- Cluttered/distracting background, busy patterns, bright walls, or a black floating-head backdrop pulling focus off your face.
- Visible edits, internal cuts, zooms, camera movement, background music, or heavy filters instead of one clean locked take.
- Overacting amplified by HD (big stage faces) — or the opposite, a flat, under-energized read.
- Camera too high (you look small) or too low/tilted up your nose — not locked at eye level.
- Wrong shot size: tiny figure in a wide room, or a suffocating extreme close-up with no air.
- Ignoring instructions: wrong format/orientation, combined when separate was asked, wrong slate placement, wrong take count, messy filenames — and submitting late, even by an hour.

## Quick Wins
- Buy a $15-50 mic and use it — clean audio is the cheapest, highest-leverage upgrade and most actors neglect it. A well-lit phone tape with a lav beats a badly-lit DSLR every time.
- Never let a window be behind you. Put the camera between you and the window so daylight hits your face — this alone fixes the most common kill-shot.
- Lock focus AND exposure before every take (iPhone: tap-and-hold for AE/AF LOCK) so the camera can't hunt or pump mid-scene.
- Get the reader (or a tape mark) right beside the lens at eye level, 6 inches off — instant believable eyeline, no faking.
- Check for a catchlight in each eye before you roll; live eyes read as present, dead eyes flatten a strong read.
- Slate separately or slate LAST on dramatic tapes so the CD hits your performance in the first few seconds (some give a tape ~4 seconds).
- Play every take back ON HEADPHONES before sending — the phone speaker hides the hum and echo that get you cut.
- Match all your light to one color temperature and set white balance — keeps skin tone truthful so you don't read 'wrong' for the role.
- Stand 3-4 feet off the backdrop to kill the hard wall-shadow that screams 'shot in a hallway.'
- Name files cleanly (FirstName_LastName_Role, underscores only) and double-check order before submitting — Eco Cast locks after send.
