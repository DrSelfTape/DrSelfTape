# Self Tape Coach — Craft Foundation (web-researched)

_Synthesized from 8 researched craft domains (Meisner, Stanislavski/objectives, indicating-vs-truth, camera technique, specific choices, comedy control, coaching redirects). 2026-06-14._

## Summary
This coach stands on one spine: acting is doing, not feeling, and reacting, not delivering. Emotion is the by-product of a specific want pursued against a real obstacle at high stakes -- never something you show -- so every redirect points the actor off themselves and onto the partner, replacing 'play sad' with an active verb aimed at the reader and 'get louder' with 'get more specific.' Because the self-tape close-up is a truth detector that magnifies every pushed expression, the philosophy is relentlessly subtractive: keep the inner intensity at one hundred percent, shrink the externals, let the thought live behind the eyes, and play the fight to suppress a feeling rather than broadcast it. Underneath the craft sit non-negotiable casting-director mechanics -- reader on the lens, clean sound, horizontal medium close-up, a committed personal choice over a safe one, and the freshest early take over a managed late one -- because the best work in the world doesn't book if the eyes, the audio, or the instructions defeat it.

## Craft Foundation
- Living truthfully under imaginary circumstances is the whole game. The circumstances are fixed by the script; your behavior inside them must be genuinely true, moment to moment. You are not showing an emotion -- you are letting something actually happen to you.
- Acting is doing, not feeling. Play an active, transitive verb aimed at the other person (to warn, to disarm, to seduce, to shame, to corner, to forgive). Emotion is the by-product of pursuing a want against an obstacle -- never the target. The instant you chase the feeling directly, you push, indicate, and fake.
- Acting is reacting. React first, then talk. The performance lives in what lands on you from your partner, not in your own line delivery. If your eyes don't change when the other person speaks, there is no scene -- only recitation.
- Get out of your head and onto your partner. The two enemies are self-consciousness and not listening. Transfer concentration off yourself and onto the other person; if you're genuinely trying to affect them, you literally cannot indicate at the same time.
- Specificity beats intensity. A vague objective ('connect,' 'be powerful') produces generic acting; precision adds fire. Name exactly who, exactly what you want from them in THIS moment, and exactly what it costs you today. The more particular the choice, the more alive and unrepeatable the work.
- Raise the stakes, not the volume. The want must be something you could actually lose; high stakes generate urgency and behavior without manufacturing emotion. But authenticity beats theatrical exaggeration -- often the highest stakes are played the quietest. Delete the word 'casual' from your vocabulary.
- Real emotion is usually suppressed, not broadcast. The audience leans toward someone fighting NOT to cry, struggling to hold it together, far more than toward someone performing the feeling at full volume. Play the resistance -- the cost, not the display.
- Work moment to moment; do not anticipate. The ending is fixed but you must not 'know' it. Each beat is discovered live by genuinely taking in the other person, so the next reaction is provoked, not produced. Reproducing your 'best take' kills spontaneity.
- Break the scene into beats and track where the tactic FAILS. Each beat gets its own verb; when charming stops working you switch to threatening, then begging. Tactic-switches are the engine of a played scene. Every line sounding the same means you aren't tracking the changes.
- Given circumstances and the moment-before are non-negotiable. Who, where, when, why, what, how -- they decide how the want is pursued. Walk in already mid-pursuit so 'action' interrupts you rather than switching you on. Arriving mid-action is the single biggest fix.
- The close-up is a truth detector. The lens reads thought, not performance, and magnifies any pushed or performed emotion. Thinking IS acting; let the thought move across the face before the line and trust the camera to catch it. Do less, mean more.
- Commit to a clear, personal point of view. Casting would rather see a definite, alive, 'wrong' choice they can redirect than a safe neutral read that shows them nothing. Specific-and-committed is castable; hedged is forgettable.
- Bold is not big or gimmicky. A bold choice is a clear, fully-committed interpretation grounded in the text -- not a louder one, and never a prop, accent, or stunt bolted on. The boldest readable choice is often total, transparent listening.
- Trust the instrument. The face is so connected to genuine inner life that it does the right thing on its own when you actually want something. Manufacturing the expression IS the indicating.

## Technique Map
- Meisner (living truthfully / repetition / acting is reacting) -- deploy when the read is anticipated, head-stuck, or the actor is waiting to talk instead of listening; retrains attention outward onto the partner so honest impulses surface even in scripted text.
- Stanislavski objectives + transitive action verbs -- deploy on a flat, recited, or dead-eyed read; replace the emotion/adjective with one active verb aimed at the partner ('to make him admit he lied') so there's something to actually DO.
- Beats + tactics (tactic-switching) -- deploy when every line sounds the same; find where the current tactic fails and the character must switch, so the scene has a journey instead of one note.
- Given circumstances + Magic If -- deploy when choices feel unspecific or float free of the situation; ground who/where/when/why and ask 'what would I do IF this were really happening to me, here, now?'
- Moment-before -- deploy when the first lines are dead and the actor warms up on camera; load what happened seconds before so 'action' interrupts a pursuit already in motion.
- Raise the stakes (Chubbuck/Stanislavski) -- deploy on a limp, low-urgency scene; push the consequence of failure from 'embarrassed' to 'mortified,' adding need and urgency without adding volume.
- Substitution / personalization / inner objects (Hagen, Chubbuck) -- deploy for emotional access when the script's circumstance doesn't move the actor; swap in a real person, memory, or mental picture so the fictional stake genuinely matters.
- Emotional preparation (Meisner) -- deploy right before rolling on a charged scene; use a private, vivid daydream to arrive emotionally alive, then DROP it and let the reader re-provoke the feeling.
- Play-the-resistance / suppression -- deploy on tear or breakdown beats that read as squeezed; play trying NOT to cry so the audience watches the fight, not the release.
- Stillness + the eyes (film technique) -- deploy when the actor is over-moving or face-pulling in a tight frame; hold the head still, fire from the eyes, and fill the stillness with a live inner event.
- Scale-down / 'less is more' (camera) -- deploy when a stage-sized choice reads as overacting on a phone-distance close-up; keep the internal intensity at 100% and shrink only the external expression.
- Flip-the-scene / hidden circumstance -- deploy when the read is the obvious, on-the-nose choice everyone makes; add a secret (guilt, leaving tomorrow), re-frame who you're talking to, or add an obstacle so the words come out fresh.
- Do-it-big-at-home -- deploy when a big emotional/comedic beat won't land truthfully; play it full-out in rehearsal so the body learns it, then bring the scaled, truthful version to the take.
- Play-the-reality-not-the-joke (comedy) -- deploy when the actor aims lines at the laugh or mugs; the character takes their problem deadly seriously and never knows they're in a comedy, so the funny falls out of sincere high-stakes pursuit.

## Self-Tape Rules
- Eyeline: place the reader at lens height, roughly 6 inches off the lens -- the reader should 'almost straddle the tripod.' A few inches too far and the camera loses the full impact of your eyes and you fall into profile.
- Never look down the barrel of the lens unless the role or instructions call for direct address -- it reads as confrontational or as a presentational pitch and breaks the fourth wall.
- Direct the reader to speak SOFTER than you (they're closer to the mic) and to support, not 'act' -- steady, prompt, lower-volume cues that protect your rhythm and levels.
- With a remote, flat, or robotic reader, supply the relationship yourself: decide exactly who they are to you ('the brother who stole from me,' not 'a man') and load a vivid substitution so the realness a flat partner can't give comes from you.
- Frame a medium close-up for drama -- mid-torso/chest to just above the head; camera at eye level or just above. Open to a 3/4 shot only when comedy or physical business needs the body.
- Shoot horizontal/landscape. Vertical reads as a TikTok, undercuts credibility, and is often auto-rejected by submission portals.
- Lock the frame and kill extraneous movement -- no panning, minimal head-bobbing; the verb works through thought and the eyes, not pacing or big gestures.
- Sound is a hard floor; lighting is forgiving. CDs tolerate average lighting but stop a tape with unclear audio in the first 10-20 seconds. Record close, clean, and un-over-processed; kill AC/traffic/pet noise and echo.
- Use a plain neutral background 2-4 feet behind you with soft, even, frontal light -- no harsh shadows or busy decor pulling focus off your face.
- Slate clearly and warmly: name (slow and unmistakable), plus agency/location/height if asked, ~10-15 seconds. It's often the only glimpse of your real personality -- no notes, no robotic delivery, no rambling life story.
- Do 3-5 takes and cap filming around one hour. Discovery lives early -- the freshest, most alive choice usually arrives by take three; later takes show you managing the performance. If torn, send the EARLIER take.
- Send only what's requested, correctly. Wrong file format/label, wrong orientation, wrong scene length, missing slate, or a late submission gets you cut before a human watches and signals you won't take direction on set.
- The first few seconds are the whole first impression: clean audio, in frame, in focus, and immediately playing the situation -- that's what stops the scroll.
- Tape multiple takes with DIFFERENT verbs on the same lines (reassure vs. warn; seduce vs. test) and pick the take where the want is most specific and the stakes highest -- not the take that is biggest.
- For a weak reader, do your emotional preparation off-camera right before you hit record, then release it and play off the reader -- don't white-knuckle the feeling through the scene.
- Scale to the lens but stay audible: avoid both the over-projected stage performance AND the inaudible 'Netflix whisper.' Keep the inner stakes huge, the surface honest, and land the words.

## Coaching Cue Bank
- INDICATING / PLAYING THE EMOTION -- Don't play sad; play trying not to let him see you cry.
- INDICATING -- Don't show me angry. Make her admit she's wrong. Go get that.
- INDICATING -- Stop telling me you're upset; show me what you DO about it.
- INDICATING -- You're playing the result. Play what comes BEFORE the result -- the trying, the holding on.
- INDICATING -- The emotion is the by-product, not the job. Chase the want and let the feeling find you.
- INDICATING -- Your eyebrows are doing the acting. Drop the face -- let me see the thought, not the illustration of it.
- INDICATING -- Trust your face. It's already doing it; the second you push, I see the push.
- DEAD EYES / FLAT READ -- What's the verb? Pick one you can DO to her: cornering, charming, or shaming. Choose one and aim it at her eyes.
- DEAD EYES -- 'To be angry' isn't playable. 'To make him admit he lied' is. Aim it at him.
- DEAD EYES -- Put the want in your eyes and send it down the lens to the reader.
- DEAD EYES -- The camera can hear you think. Just think the thought and let the lens come get it.
- DEAD EYES -- Keep the head still and fire from your eyes.
- NOT LISTENING -- Stop acting and just listen. Let the line actually land before you answer.
- NOT LISTENING -- You're not listening, you're waiting. Let what she says actually hit you and knock you somewhere new.
- NOT LISTENING -- Acting is reacting. React first, then talk.
- NOT LISTENING -- Let it land before you answer. Take the moment -- the silence is the scene.
- NOT LISTENING -- You already know what's coming; I can see it. Let it surprise you this take.
- PUSHING THE BIG BEAT -- Raise the stakes, not the volume. Same want, twice as much to lose, half as loud.
- PUSHING -- You jumped to the blow-up. What's the last second you could keep it in? Don't cross the line until it forces you across.
- PUSHING -- You're pushing. You don't have to make me feel it -- you have to do something about it.
- PUSHING -- That was a stage choice. The back row is six inches away now; bring it down to a whisper of itself.
- PUSHING -- Try the opposite: you're furious, so hold it down. Let it leak, don't pour it.
- GENERIC CHOICE -- That's a generic choice. Who in YOUR life would make you feel exactly this? Play it to them.
- GENERIC CHOICE -- That choice is fine. It's also the first choice everyone makes. Give me the one underneath it.
- GENERIC CHOICE -- Who is that to you, specifically? Not 'my boss' -- give me the exact face. Now say the line to that face.
- GENERIC CHOICE -- You've got a secret she doesn't know -- you're leaving tomorrow. Play the whole scene over that.
- GENERIC CHOICE -- Make the wrong choice boldly. I can always pull you back, but I can't direct nothing.
- GENERIC CHOICE -- What just happened, three seconds before the camera rolled? Start there. Let 'action' interrupt you.
- MUGGING COMEDY -- Don't play the joke; play the problem. What do you actually need from this person right now?
- MUGGING COMEDY -- He doesn't know it's funny. To him this is a five-alarm fire. Treat it like one.
- MUGGING COMEDY -- You're aiming at me for the laugh. Aim at HIM for what you need. The laugh isn't yours to get.
- MUGGING COMEDY -- That was a face. Give me a thought instead -- let me watch you realize it.
- MUGGING COMEDY -- Land it and let it breathe. Hold the last look; don't run from the button.
- MUGGING COMEDY -- Be EXTREMELY this, but really this. Push the real anxiety to ten; don't perform 'anxious.'
- MUGGING COMEDY -- Say the writer's words in the writer's order. The rhythm is already built; don't remodel it.
- CHARM-RESET -- That was charming. Now stop being likable and just need something from him.
- CHARM-RESET -- You're smoothing every beat into the same pleasant tone. Make yourself WANT something uncomfortable from her.
- STAKES / URGENCY -- What do you lose if you don't get this? Make it specific and make it cost something -- then get more focused, not louder.
- STAKES -- Delete the word 'casual.' Nothing here is casual to you -- this matters RIGHT NOW.
- SELF-CONSCIOUSNESS -- Take the heat off yourself. Put 100% of your attention on the other person and let yourself be affected.
- SELF-CONSCIOUSNESS -- You're in your head. Get your eyes off your own performance and onto the reader; let them affect you.
- DO THE ACTION -- Stop performing it FOR me. Do it TO her.
- DO THE ACTION -- Don't do anything unless something makes you do it. What made you say that?
- PREP -- Prepare before I roll, then drop it. The reader's job is to bring it back up; don't carry it yourself.
- BEATS -- Every line sounds the same. Where does she find out something she didn't know? That has to land and change what you do next.
- EYELINE -- Put your reader on the lens. Right now I'm watching the side of your face -- I need both your eyes.
- SCALE -- Do less. The camera's six inches from your face; the thought is enough.
- STILLNESS -- Don't perform the pause. Live in it.
- MAGIC IF -- If this were really happening to you, right now, with everything on the line -- what would you actually do? Do that.
