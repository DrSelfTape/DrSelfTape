# Self-Tape Acting Knowledge Base — AI Coaching Brain

## What This Is

This document is the craft-and-community half of the Dr Self Tapes AI coaching brain. It synthesizes web research across Reddit's r/acting, named casting directors (Marci Liroff, Heidi Miami Marshall, Erica S. Bream, Amy Jo Berman, Paul Weber), working acting coaches, and technical production guides into one operational coaching doctrine. Where the language below is prescriptive ("do less," "play an action, not an emotion," "reader to the lens"), it reflects broad, cross-source consensus rather than any single opinion.

It is designed to pair with its empirical companion, **`selftape-research-study.md`** — the per-clip analysis of a rig-controlled corpus of 110 real Dr Self Tapes tapes across ~25 actors and seven tone registers. That study is the *evidence*: what choices actually scored well on real tapes shot on a controlled rig. This document is the *theory*: the named craft principles and CD psychology that explain *why*. The cross-reference section at the end maps where the two agree (they agree on the load-bearing rules with striking consistency) and where the web wisdom adds things a fixed-rig corpus structurally couldn't reveal (audio, slate format, file hygiene, submission etiquette, mindset).

The throughline of the entire brain: **casting is buying a thought behind the eyes and a real reaction to another person. Everything else — framing, light, slate, file name — exists to get out of the way of that.**

## High-Confidence Rules

These are the most-agreed-upon, highest-value rules across every source — the AI's core coaching doctrine. Each is near-universal consensus; treat them as load-bearing.

1. **Make one specific, fully-committed choice** no one else would make — a strong "wrong" choice beats a safe nothing, because casting can redirect commitment but can do nothing with vague.
2. **"Bold" means specific and clear, not bigger and louder** — on camera, subtlety and a distinct point of view land harder than volume.
3. **Play an action, not an emotion** — boil the scene to "I want ___ from ___," pursue it with active transitive verbs, and let feeling arise as a byproduct.
4. **Don't indicate or act the result** — never pre-decide a line's emotional outcome; pursue the action under pressure and let the emotion surprise you in real time.
5. **Listen and receive before you respond** — let the partner's words land and change something in you; "waiting for your cue" is the single most common reason a flat tape fails.
6. **Play an arc, not a state** — change tactics on the beats so even a short scene travels through 3–4 earned colors; single-note reads are forgettable.
7. **Raise and name the stakes** — decide exactly what's lost and put a ticking clock on it; if the script gives no urgency, invent a concrete consequence.
8. **Do less; restraint over pushing** — the HD lens amplifies, so the small truthful take books; but "grounded" still means *alive*, never blank.
9. **Win the first 10–15 seconds** — arrive already mid-life on a clear "moment before"; a cold neutral open wastes the most-watched moment of the tape.
10. **Reader as close to the lens as physically possible**, eyeline ~6 inches off-lens at eye level — wide placement throws you into profile and reads theatrical.
11. **Never look down the barrel in the scene** (only the slate) — into-lens reads as a news anchor, not a film moment.
12. **Use a live, varied reader balanced below your volume** — never tape into silence; a monotone or overpowering reader collapses the scene.
13. **Prioritize sound over picture** — it's the #1 technical killer; clean, quiet, external-mic audio beats an expensive camera every time.
14. **Light from the front, soft and even; never backlight** — casting is buying micro-expressions a silhouette hides.
15. **Shoot landscape 16:9, eye-level lens, locked-off tripod, MCU frame** with ~1 inch headroom — vertical, shake, and headroom errors are instant amateur tells.
16. **Submit ONE continuous take per scene, head/tail trimmed, no music/effects/props** — auditions have no cuts; flourishes signal you misread the format.
17. **The breakdown overrides every default** — instruction-following is a direct proxy for on-set reliability; some offices won't even open a tape that ignored directions.
18. **Protect spontaneity** — the first 3–4 takes are usually the best; the unlimited-retake format is a trap that sands the life out of a performance.

## Performance & Craft Principles

This section governs the acting itself — the choices, the inner life, the moment-to-moment behavior the camera captures. An AI self-tape coach applies these to diagnose *why* a read feels flat, forced, or forgettable, and to give the actor one concrete, actionable adjustment. Notes should always be specific, kind, and tied to the next take. Principles marked **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]** are near-universal consensus across casting directors, named coaches, and the actor community — treat them as load-bearing.

---

### 1. Make a specific, bold choice — and commit fully **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]**

**Rule:** Make one specific, fully-committed choice no one else in the pile would make, grounded in what the text actually gives you. "Bold" means a clear point of view and stakes, not louder or wackier. A strong "wrong" choice beats a safe, neutral nothing — because casting can redirect a committed actor but can do nothing with a vague one.

**Why:** Casting directors watch hundreds of tapes; the remembered actors aren't the most technically clean, they're the ones who committed to a distinctive, justified choice. A please-everyone read signals you didn't understand the scene and gives the room nothing to remember. Commitment reveals imagination, preparation, and a personal point of view. (Marci Liroff, CSA; Backstage; *About The Work Actors Studio*.) Note the dissent worth honoring: coach Paul Weber warns "make bold choices" is constantly *misread* as "perform bigger" — on camera, specificity and subtlety land harder than volume.

**How the AI phrases the note:**
- "Your read is clean but safe — it could be anyone. Pick one specific point of view on this scene and commit to it all the way, even if it feels like a risk. They can always pull you back; they can't do anything with neutral."
- "Don't confuse bold with loud. Find the *unexpected but true* way into this line — a strange calm where I'd expect anger, a private joke under the threat."
- "Lock one or two concrete details before your next take: a secret this character is keeping, exactly who the other person is to you. Let that color how you move and speak."

---

### 2. Play an action and a clear objective — not an emotion **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]**

**Rule:** Boil the scene to one sentence — *what you want, from whom.* Phrase the objective as something you want *from* the other person, then pursue it with active transitive verbs (to warn, to seduce, to corner, to disarm) rather than playing a feeling. The objective dictates the rhythm, inflection, and tone of every line for you. Changing *tactics* in pursuit of that one want is what creates dynamics.

**Why:** Pursuing a want generates real behavior and stops you "playing the result." An objective aimed at the scene partner keeps the performance relational and alive instead of self-focused. A single clear want organizes every beat; an over-analyzed scene with no central objective plays muddy. (Backstage; *Daily Actor*; *Off Book*.)

**How the AI phrases the note:**
- "Before the next take, finish this sentence out loud: 'In this scene I want ___ from ___.' Then chase it. Don't play 'sad' — play *trying to make her stay.*"
- "Swap your objective for an active verb you can actually *do* to the other person. Not 'be angry' — *to corner him*. Watch how it changes your rhythm on its own."
- "You're playing the mood of the scene instead of the goal. Pick the want and let the mood take care of itself."

---

### 3. Live the emotion — don't indicate it **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]**

**Rule:** Don't play sadness, anger, or fear directly. Pick what the character is *trying to do* about the feeling (hold onto the relationship, hide the truth, win the argument) and let the emotion arise as a byproduct of that struggle. Don't act the *result* — never pre-decide the emotional outcome of a line and then perform toward it. Pursue the action and let the feeling happen to you in real time.

**Why:** Forcing an emotional display produces indicating — visible effort, pushed intensity, extra emphasis to *show* casting you're feeling something — which reads as forced and untrue. Pursuing an action under pressure produces truthful emotion the camera reads as real. "Result acting" telegraphs the ending of the beat and kills surprise; CDs spot a performance chasing a predetermined emotion instantly. This is the core anti-indicating fix. (StageMilk; Maggie Flanigan Studio; Amy Jo Berman, former HBO casting.)

**How the AI phrases the note:**
- "I can see you *working* to feel it — that effort is reading as the performance. Stop trying to feel; pick what the character is trying to *do* about the feeling, and let the emotion sneak up on you."
- "You arrived at this line having already decided it ends in tears. Drop the destination. Fight for what you want and let the take surprise you."
- "Don't show me sad. Try to *hide* how much this hurts while you ask for what you need — the hiding is what will move me."

---

### 4. Build a within-take arc with real gear-changes **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]**

**Rule:** Know where the character starts emotionally and where they end, and let a discovery or turn happen in the middle so you're not at one pitch the whole scene. Identify the **beats** — the points where something the partner says or does forces you to change tactic — and let your approach visibly shift at each one (charm fails, so you threaten; the threat fails, so you plead).

**Why:** A flat, single-note read is forgettable; tracking a beginning-middle-end shift gives even a short scene a journey and shows range in seconds. Changing tactics in pursuit of the same objective is what prevents a monotone read — each beat-change is a new color for the CD to see. (Backstage; Utah Shakespeare Festival; *Spacious Acting*.)

**How the AI phrases the note:**
- "Right now the scene plays at one temperature start to finish. Mark the moment your first tactic *fails* — that's where you change gears. Charm didn't work, so now what?"
- "Map three beats: where you start, the thing they say that turns you, and where you land. Let me *see* the turn happen on your face."
- "You're winning too easily. Find the point where your approach stops working and you have to try something new — that shift is the whole scene."

---

### 5. Listen, receive, and react — don't wait for your cue **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]**

**Rule:** Put your energy on your partner, not on your own performance. Actually listen and let the meaning of what they say *land and change something in you* before you respond — receiving is internal, mere reacting is external. When your attention drifts, lock onto a tiny physical detail of your partner to pull focus back outward. Make the imaginary partner specifically *real*: picture exactly who they are and what they mean to you, and the behavior follows on its own.

**Why:** It is always obvious on camera when an actor is just waiting to talk rather than responding — most flat self-tapes fail right here. Genuine connection and warmth with the reader separates you from the pile more than any technical polish; casting is largely judging whether you can act *opposite* someone and sell the relationship. Letting the words do something to you produces the involuntary micro-reactions a close-up lives on. This is the Meisner foundation — "the reality of doing." (StageMilk's "900+ tapes"; Sanford Meisner / Maggie Flanigan Studio; Heidi Miami Marshall; Simon Rajala.)

**How the AI phrases the note:**
- "You're loading your next line while they're still talking — I can see it. Let what they say actually hit you and change something before you answer."
- "Stop performing *at* the reader and start listening *to* them. Decide exactly who this person is to you, then really take in what they say."
- "Don't react to the line — *receive* it. Let it land, let it cost you something, then respond from there. That tiny delay is where the truth lives."

---

### 6. Raise and clarify the stakes **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]**

**Rule:** Before you tape, name exactly what the character stands to lose in this scene, invent a concrete consequence, and give it a ticking clock (e.g. "she's getting married tomorrow"). Treat the scene as figuratively life-or-death — the character's most important day. If the script doesn't hand you urgency, manufacture it.

**Why:** Specific, high stakes give the lines urgency and purpose; vague stakes make even a technically clean tape feel flat and low-energy. Specific consequences plus a deadline manufacture genuine drive. (Denise Simon; NYFA; Spotlight; Backstage.)

**How the AI phrases the note:**
- "This read is technically clean but there's no urgency. Answer this before the next take: what *exactly* do you lose if this conversation fails? Now play like that loss is one wrong sentence away."
- "Give it a clock. Decide there's a deadline — she leaves in an hour, the offer expires tonight — and let that pressure into your voice."
- "Right now nothing's at risk, so nothing lands. Raise the stakes to life-or-death in your imagination, even if the words are casual."

---

### 7. Do less — restraint and stillness over pushing **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]**

**Rule:** Scale the performance down to grounded, conversational realism — under-controlled reactions, small emotional shifts, natural pacing. On a close-up, pare it down until it almost feels like nothing — *just be.* Let controlled stillness do the work: don't fidget, don't pad silences with gestures, and don't drop into the flat "Netflix whisper" either. Don't rush the dialogue — pace is where the listening and reactions live. **Caveat:** restraint only works when there's a real inner life underneath; "grounded" still means *alive and active*, never blank.

**Why:** The HD lens magnifies every facial movement, so theatrical intensity reads as overacting and dated; the small, truthful take is what books. What feels like under-doing it to the actor reads as truthful and confident on screen. Controlled stillness reads as command; fidgeting reads as nerves. But under-acting — withholding all energy so nothing reads — is as damaging as overacting. (Michael Caine; allcasting CD panel; NYCastings; David Genik for the caveat.)

**How the AI phrases the note:**
- "The camera is already amplifying you — you don't have to push. Cut the effort in half and let the thought do the work. On a close-up, almost-nothing reads as everything."
- "Your hands and face are working overtime; it's reading as nerves. Get still, hold the thought, and trust that I can see it."
- "Careful — you dialed it so far down it went blank. 'Grounded' isn't 'absent.' Keep the volume low but the *want* burning hot underneath."
- "You're rushing to get it over with, and it's flattening every beat. Slow down enough to actually hear the reader and let the lines land."

---

### 8. Be present — start alive, stay alive, and let yourself get lost in it **[HIGH-CONFIDENCE]**

**Rule:** Play a clear **"moment before"** — arrive in the take already mid-life, carrying where the character was emotionally ten seconds before the scene began. Don't open from a cold, neutral blank; come in already looking at your partner and take the beat before the first line. Hold an extra beat of stillness at the end — breathe, stay in it, don't rush to cut. Tell the story with your eyes: a genuine thought running behind them reads as a deep well of feeling with no added facial effort. Then stop self-managing — make the choice, get present, and let yourself get lost in the moment.

**Why:** Casting often decides in the first 10-15 seconds, so a flat open wastes the most-watched moment of the tape; a tape that feels mid-life signals craft instantly. The close-up captures *thought* — an actor genuinely thinking the character's thought is compelling with the face nearly still, while an actor "doing" an expression looks false. A held breath at the end signals patience and control (one CD: it "told me everything... she booked it"). The format's unlimited retakes are a trap — endless re-records sand the life out of a performance; the magic is in committing and being *in it*. (StageMilk; NYCastings; Michael Caine; Acting Studio Chicago CDs; Casting Networks.)

**How the AI phrases the note:**
- "You started from zero and the first few seconds — the ones they're most likely to watch — went to waste. Decide what just happened ten seconds ago and walk in already carrying it."
- "Let a real, specific thought run behind your eyes. You don't have to *do* anything with your face — if you're genuinely thinking it, I'll feel it."
- "Don't rush to cut. Hold the last beat, breathe, stay in it one more second. That stillness reads as confidence and instinct."
- "You're managing the take instead of living in it. Make your choice, then stop watching yourself — get lost in the scene and let it be a little messy and alive."

---

### Coaching meta-rules for applying the above

- **One note at a time.** Give the actor a single, concrete adjustment per take — never a list. Stacked notes paralyze.
- **Diagnose the root, not the symptom.** "Flat" is usually *no objective* (Principle 2) or *no stakes* (Principle 6). "Forced/fake" is usually *indicating* (Principle 3) or *pushing* (Principle 7). "Forgettable" is usually *no specific choice* (Principle 1) or *no arc* (Principle 4). "Dead behind the eyes" is usually *not listening* (Principle 5) or *not present* (Principle 8).
- **Protect spontaneity.** The best work is usually in the first 3-4 takes; aliveness evaporates with repetition. Warn an actor who is over-recording before the life sands off.
- **Specificity beats complexity.** When in doubt, the highest-yield note is almost always "make it more *specific*" — a specific want, a specific partner, a specific consequence, a specific thought.

## Technical & Production Standards (incl. Slate & Submission)

These are the concrete, checkable standards an AI coach can grade a self-tape against. Rules marked **[CONSENSUS]** are agreed on across casting directors, named coaches, and the r/acting community — treat them as high-confidence and flag any violation firmly. Items marked **[GENRE/JUDGMENT]** are situational; coach them as defaults, not absolutes.

> **The override rule** — read first. **[CONSENSUS]** Whatever the casting breakdown says wins over everything below. Orientation, slate format, file naming, number of takes, frame signals, wardrobe notes — follow them to the letter. Every office asks for something slightly different, so never reuse the last office's rules. CDs (Marci Liroff, Heidi Marshall, SAG-AFTRA panels) treat instruction-following as a direct proxy for on-set reliability; some offices won't even open a tape that ignored directions. *AI check: if the user supplied a breakdown, validate against it first and surface conflicts before applying any default here.*

---

### 1. Framing & Shot Size

- **Default frame: medium close-up — mid-chest (roughly mid-bicep) to a sliver of headroom above the head, body centered, eyes on the upper-third line. [CONSENSUS]** This is the standard "self-tape frame." It captures the micro-expressions casting is buying without losing body language. Going too tight strangles the read; too wide loses the eyes. (StageMilk, Erica S. Bream CSA, Backstage.)
- **Headroom: about one inch — never crop the top of the head, never leave a strip of empty "sky" above it. [CONSENSUS]** Headroom errors are an instant amateur tell. *AI check: flag both scalp-crop and excess headroom.*
- **Let genre widen the frame. [GENRE/JUDGMENT]** For comedy, theatre, or physical material, frame looser — a 3/4 "cowboy shot" (bottom edge around mid-thigh) so the body and gestures have room. Tight framing produces tight performances; comedy needs to breathe. (Heidi Miami Marshall.)
- **Composition: default to centered.** **[GENRE/JUDGMENT]** Dead-center is always safe. The 2/3 rule (body in one third, eyeline crossing to the open side) is an acceptable alternative, but if unsure, center it. A lopsided frame with the eyeline running off the near edge reads cramped. (Heidi Marshall.)

### 2. Camera, Resolution & Stability

- **Shoot horizontally (landscape, 16:9) — always, unless the breakdown specifies vertical. [CONSENSUS]** Casting reviews in a horizontal player that mimics film/TV; vertical reads like a TikTok clip and costs credibility. (Universal across CDs, StageMilk, SAG-AFTRA, Acting Magazine.)
- **Camera at exact eye level, or a hair above — never below. [CONSENSUS]** A level lens reads as natural eye-to-eye contact. Shooting up flares the nostrils and feels confrontational; shooting down shrinks and diminishes you. Stack books or raise the tripod to hit it. (StageMilk, LSTFI/Strasberg, multiple CDs.)
- **Mount on a tripod or stand — never handheld, no zooms, no roaming. [CONSENSUS]** One locked, stationary frame for the whole take. Any shake reads as careless, and casting equates production sloppiness with how you'll behave on set. (SAG-AFTRA, Actors Access, dazzlerr.)
- **Use the rear (back) camera, not the front-facing/selfie cam. [CONSENSUS]** The rear sensor is higher-resolution and sharper. (Actor Aesthetic, SAG-AFTRA.)
- **Resolution: cap at 1080p (720p is a safe, highly compatible alternative) at 24, 25, or 30 fps. Avoid 4K. [CONSENSUS]** 4K bloats the file with no casting benefit and routinely fails casting-server upload limits; film-standard frame rates look professional. But don't over-compress either — grain and noise that look fine on a phone become glaring on the large monitors casting uses. (Puro Casting, Mighty Tripod, CastMeNow.)

### 3. Lighting

- **Light the face from the FRONT — key light or window behind the camera, facing you. Never backlight. [CONSENSUS]** Casting needs to read every subtle expression; a bright window or lamp behind you silhouettes you and throws the face into shadow. You need more light than pixels — a cheap, even light beats a pricier camera. (Spotlight, The Modern Actor, StageMilk, Marci Liroff.)
- **Even, soft, diffused light across face and torso. [CONSENSUS]** Avoid the half-lit/shadowed face unless the genre truly calls for it. Diffuse a bare bulb with a white pillowcase, baking paper, or a softbox/LED panel; hard light creates unflattering dark edges and hot spots. (StageMilk, Bohemian's Minds.)
- **A symmetric two-lamp setup (one diffused light each side of the camera, at eye level) is the safe no-budget rig. [CONSENSUS]** Bounce a fill with a cheap white board opposite your key to lift shadows. **[GENRE/JUDGMENT]** (Greg Sims, No Film School.)
- **Avoid overhead/ceiling light as your main source. [CONSENSUS]** Top-down light casts "raccoon" shadows under the eyes and chin, aging and hollowing the face. Bring the key down toward eye level. (Casting Frontier, Hunter Talent.)
- **Ring-light caution. [GENRE/JUDGMENT]** A ring light beats no light, but as a sole key it stamps a visible ring catchlight in the eyes that reads amateur. Prefer soft, diffused, even sources. (Spotlight.)
- **Glasses: raise the key higher and angle it down/off to the side until the lens reflection disappears. [GENRE/JUDGMENT]** Glare hides the eyes — the single most important read. (No Film School.)

*AI check: no part of the background should be brighter than the performer's face; flag a visibly backlit or shadowed face.*

### 4. Sound & Audio

- **Prioritize sound over picture — it is the #1 technical killer. [CONSENSUS]** Casting will tolerate average lighting but stops the tape the instant dialogue is unclear, before the acting is even judged. First-round reviews happen on laptops and headphones, not theatre speakers. (allcasting CD panel, Erica S. Bream CSA, Spotlight, StageMilk.)
- **Record in the quietest room you can and kill every hum first. [CONSENSUS]** Turn off fans, A/C, fridge; mute all device notifications; avoid rooms near traffic or pets. Listen for the ambient noise you tune out daily — reshoot or relocate if you hear it. Low-frequency hum can't be fully removed in post, and over-processed noise filters sound as bad as the noise. (Spotlight, StageMilk, allcasting.)
- **Tape in a room with soft furnishings — carpet, curtains, upholstery — to kill echo. [CONSENSUS]** Hard, bare-walled rooms create reverb that muddies the voice. (Everything Cinema, Backstage.)
- **Use an external mic, not the phone's built-in. [CONSENSUS]** A clip-on lav (e.g. RØDE smartLav+) or on-camera shotgun (RØDE VideoMic), roughly 6–12 inches from the performer, captures clean, present dialogue. Built-in phone mics grab room ambience and sound thin. (Backstage, Actor CEO.)
- **Balance the reader's volume against yours. [CONSENSUS]** Sit the reader a few feet back from the mic and bring their level down so they don't record 20× louder than you and step on your lines — the most-cited sound flaw. (Erica S. Bream CSA.)

### 5. Background

- **Plain, muted, single-color, mid-tone background — light grey, beige, cream, pale blue, or soft green/navy. [CONSENSUS]** Strip gallery walls, posters, cords, fridges, TVs, laundry, and pets. A smooth neutral bedsheet or pop-up backdrop works if you lack a clean wall. Bright reds/oranges/yellows distract; busy frames scream amateur and pull focus from the face. Avoid digital/virtual backgrounds. (Heidi Marshall, The Modern Actor, allcasting, StageMilk.)
- **Stand 2–3 feet off the wall. [CONSENSUS]** Separation prevents hard cast shadows and adds subtle depth — the cheap stand-in for a backlight. (Erica S. Bream CSA, Bohemian's Minds.)
- **Never wear a color that matches the backdrop. [CONSENSUS]** Matching wardrobe makes you blend into the wall. (Marci Liroff, SAG-AFTRA.)

### 6. Eyeline & Reader Placement

This is the single most-repeated technical note in self-taping. Treat all of the following as high-confidence.

- **Place the reader as close to the lens as physically possible — almost straddling the tripod, just clearing the lens. [CONSENSUS]** If the reader sits even a few inches too wide, your eyes pull off-camera, you fall into near-profile, and the read "plays to the room" theatrically instead of cinematically. (Marci Liroff CSA, Heidi Marshall, StageMilk, SAG-AFTRA — universal.)
- **Keep the eyeline tight: about 6 inches to one side of the lens, at eye level. [CONSENSUS]** This keeps both eyes visible and the connection believable.
- **Never look directly down the barrel during the scene — unless the breakdown explicitly asks. [CONSENSUS]** Looking into the lens breaks the fourth wall, reads as a commercial/news-anchor/piece-to-camera choice, and unsettles the viewer. The camera should be a fly-on-the-wall to a real scene. (The slate is the exception — see §8.)
- **Set the reader at the actor's eye level and match posture. [CONSENSUS]** If you sit, the reader sits; if you stand, they stand. Mismatched heights create a low/high angle that distorts the character's status and power dynamic. (Heidi Marshall, StageMilk.)
- **Lock 2–3 simple eyelines and keep each consistent. [CONSENSUS]** Once a character is "placed" in a spot, don't let your eyes drift to a new spot for them mid-scene. Wandering eyes look untrained and confuse who's speaking and where. (StageMilk eyelines guide.)
- **Remote (Zoom/FaceTime) readers: position their window just off-lens and work extra hard for connection. [GENRE/JUDGMENT]** Heidi Marshall calls remote reading the closest thing to green-screen acting — without deliberate effort the eyeline drifts wide and the performance goes cold.

*AI check: if the user can describe or show reader position, flag any placement more than ~1 foot from the lens, any straight-into-lens scene eyeline, and any inconsistent eyeline across beats.*

### 7. Wardrobe

- **Suggest the character, don't costume it. [CONSENSUS]** Imagine the full costume and dial it back 50–70% — a V-neck hints scrubs, a blazer reads CEO/lawyer. A literal costume reads as trying too hard. **[GENRE/JUDGMENT]** on how far to lean. (Casting Networks.)
- **Wear solid, mid-tone colors that contrast the background. [CONSENSUS]**
- **Avoid: pure white, all-black, bright red/neons, patterns, stripes, logos, text, and anything shiny. [CONSENSUS]** White and black fool the camera's exposure and crush detail; red bleeds and vibrates; patterns/stripes moiré and steal focus; logos and text distract. (Casting Networks, Acting Magazine, Nick Dunning.)
- **Be camera-ready overall — appropriate hair and makeup, well-rested. [CONSENSUS]** Treat it as walking into the office. Physical prep reinforces "this is a real audition," and the camera reads your self-belief. (Mel Mack Acting Studio, Margie Haber.)

### 8. Slating

- **Only slate if casting asks, and follow their exact requested format. [CONSENSUS]**
- **Keep it short: name alone ~5 seconds; with height/location ~10–15 seconds total. Don't pad it. [CONSENSUS]** The slate is setup, not the audition. (allcasting, Acting Magazine, MasterClass.)
- **Include only the requested info. The common default set is NAME, HEIGHT, and where you're taping from (location); add AGE only if under 18. [CONSENSUS]** Don't volunteer agency, credits, or weight unless asked. (StarNow, Backstage.)
- **For the slate ONLY, look directly into the lens; then move the eyeline off-lens for the scene. [CONSENSUS]** Hold the lens 1–2 seconds before saying your name, with held eye contact — not a flicker-glance, which reads as low confidence. (Backstage, Weeble Mountain, Needacrew.)
- **Slate energy: roughly "half you, half the character" — warm, present, in your real voice, tone-matched to the project (warm for comedy, grounded for drama). [CONSENSUS]** The slate is the only glimpse of your real personality and the first read on castability; a flat "name and rank" wastes it, a forced grin reads anxious. Marci Liroff cites Lucy Fry's relaxed, character-tinged slate as the gold standard. CDs can instantly hear an "audition voice." (Marci Liroff, Heidi Marshall, Casting Networks.)
- **Be still during the slate, especially hands. [CONSENSUS]** If you can't be still for the slate, casting assumes you can't be still on camera. (SAG-AFTRA, Acting Studio Chicago.)
- **Slate once per submission — do NOT re-slate at the top of every take. [CONSENSUS]** After the slate, go straight into the first line — no restating your name, no reading the scene description aloud, no spoken "action" or countdown. (Weeble Mountain, Heidi Marshall, Backstage.)
- **Default to a tail slate (slate at the end) — but only if instructions don't specify. [CONSENSUS]** A tail slate lets casting see your performance first and keeps the slate's neutral energy from diluting your opening beat. Cut directly from the last scene to the slate. Always defer to explicit instructions. (Weeble Mountain, CD community.)
- **Know the slate vocabulary. [CONSENSUS]** A *traditional* slate frames mid-chest to top of head; a *full-body* slate shows head to toe; *profiles* means turn your whole body left and right (push long hair back so the profile is clear). For a full-body slate, frame wide for the slate then move in so the scene plays from roughly mid-chest up — never perform the scene at full-body distance, which hides facial detail. (Coach Kimberly Crandall, Heidi Marshall, Spotlight.)

### 9. Editing, Length & Submission Mechanics

**Editing**
- **One continuous take per scene — no internal edits. [CONSENSUS]** Auditions have no cuts; a Frankenstein-edited "perfect" read shows editing skill, not acting, and casting can tell. Do 10 takes if you must, but submit one clean run. (StageMilk, Sien Josephine Casting.)
- **Trim head and tail tightly — the file is JUST the scene. [CONSENSUS]** Cut pressing record, walking into frame, throat-clearing, belt-fixing, and the drift-off after the last line. Start just before the first beat, end just after the last, leaving only a 1–2 second buffer each side so audio doesn't clip. (StageMilk, SelfTaper, Casting Networks.)
- **No production flourishes. [CONSENSUS]** No background music, fades, star wipes, glitter flares, page-curls, or transitions. Casting is grading the performance, not a short film; any flourish reads as misunderstanding the format. (CastMeNow, Actor Career Center.)
- **No props/blocking/pantomime unless the script specifically requires it and it's simple (pen, watch, bag). [CONSENSUS]** No weapons, no plush toy as a baby, no random props to seem "interesting." A gun toward the lens hijacks attention entirely; adding props signals you're not confident in your choices. Don't self-direct camera moves either. (StageMilk, Marci Liroff.)

**Length**
- **Keep it tight: roughly 1–2 minutes per scene; monologues under ~90 seconds; total under ~3–5 minutes for multiple scenes. [CONSENSUS]** Casting dismisses most tapes in the first 10–20 seconds, so overstaying loses them. **[GENRE/JUDGMENT]** rough scaling by role size: co-star 30s–1.5min, guest/recurring 2–3min, lead/series-regular up to ~5min. Leave them wanting more. (Acting Magazine.)

**Number of takes**
- **Submit ONE take per scene unless you have two genuinely different interpretations. [CONSENSUS]** Two should be distinct *choices* (e.g. warm/lighthearted vs. guarded/hostile), not a marginally cleaner line reading. Near-identical takes read as indecision and waste the CD's time. **[GENRE/JUDGMENT]** for small/minor roles, two max — never three or four. (StageMilk, Weeble Mountain, Acting Magazine.)
- **Send separate take files unless told to combine. [CONSENSUS]** Most CDs want individual files, not one edited reel. (Heidi Marshall.)

**File naming & format**
- **Name the file plainly and consistently: PerformerName_Project_Role_Scene (e.g. `TRUEROMANCE_Alabama_PatriciaArquette_Scene1Take1`). [CONSENSUS]** Letters, numbers, underscores only — no special characters that break casting-platform uploaders. A default camera name like `IMG_0423.mov` gets lost; mislabeled files are a real CD pet peeve and can get a tape skipped before a frame is watched. (Heidi Marshall, Marci Liroff, Spotlight, SelfTaper.)
- **Export as MP4 (H.264), reasonably compressed, under ~500 MB. [CONSENSUS]** MP4 is universally playable; oversized files time out, fail to upload, or won't preview. (Spotlight, SelfTaper.)

**Sharing & etiquette**
- **If submitting via link, host on Vimeo or YouTube set to "Unlisted" — not "Private." [CONSENSUS]** "Private" blocks the CD or triggers access-request friction; a link a CD can't open is a missed audition. (SelfTaper, Vimeo.)
- **Submit before the deadline — ideally early. [CONSENSUS]** Late tapes frequently go unwatched once the list closes. **[GENRE/JUDGMENT]** Submitting early also gives the CD room to send a redirect before the cutoff, turning a near-miss into a booking. Build a fast, repeatable setup so you can turn a tape around in hours, not days. (allcasting, SAG-AFTRA, Casting Frontier, Mel Mack.)
- **Don't send unsolicited role-specific tapes you weren't invited to read for. [GENRE/JUDGMENT]** Casting works from a submitted/agent-channeled list; cold-sending a role tape reads as not understanding the process. (Erica S. Bream CSA.)

## Common Mistakes & Red Flags (the AI should flag these)

Each item below is a **detectable red flag** paired with the **corrective coaching note** the AI should deliver. Items marked **[CONSENSUS]** are agreed on across Reddit, named casting directors (Marci Liroff, Erica S. Bream, Heidi Marshall), and technical guides — treat these as high-confidence, non-negotiable rules. Items marked **[CONTESTED]** are sound advice from fewer sources or genre-dependent — offer them as guidance, not law.

---

### A. Eyeline & Camera Connection

**Wide reader placement / eyeline drifting into profile** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor's eyes are angled noticeably off to one side; head turns away from the lens; viewer reads near-profile.
*Note:* "Move your reader right next to the lens — almost straddling the tripod, within ~6 inches of the camera and at your eye level. A wide reader throws your eyes off-camera and reads theatrical, like you're talking to someone who isn't in the room." This is the single most-repeated self-tape note across every source (Marci Liroff, Heidi Marshall, StageMilk's 900+ tapes).

**Looking straight down the barrel of the lens (in the scene)** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* eyeline locked dead-center into the lens during the scene (not the slate), with no breakdown instruction to do so.
*Note:* "Keep your eyeline just *off* the lens. Looking into it breaks the fourth wall and reads as a news anchor or piece-to-camera, not a film moment. Only look down the barrel if the breakdown explicitly asks." (Exception: the slate, where direct-to-lens is correct.)

**Inconsistent / wandering eyelines** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor's eyes flit to different spots for the same off-camera character, or drift around between lines.
*Note:* "Plot 2–3 simple eyelines before you record and lock each one. Once a character is 'placed,' keep them there — drifting eyes confuse who's speaking and read as untrained."

**Camera height off (angled up or down)** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* up-the-nose nostril angle (camera too low) or a looking-down distortion (camera too high).
*Note:* "Set the lens at exact eye level, matched to your reader's eye height. A low angle is unflattering and confrontational; a high angle shrinks you. Stack books or raise the tripod to hit eye level."

---

### B. Audio (the #1 technical killer)

**Unclear or muddy dialogue in the first few seconds** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* speech is hard to make out, thin, distant, or buried.
*Note:* "Prioritize sound over picture. Casting reviews on laptops and headphones, and they'll stop the tape the second they can't hear you. Use a quiet room and a simple external/lav mic 6–12 inches away — clean audio beats an expensive camera." (Erica S. Bream, allcasting, StageMilk all rank this as the fastest rejection.)

**Ambient hum, echo, or background noise** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* AC/fridge/fan hum, traffic, a barking dog, or reverb from a bare hard-walled room.
*Note:* "Kill every hum before rolling — fans, A/C, fridge, notifications. Record in a room with soft furnishings (carpet, curtains) to absorb echo. Over-processed noise filters are as bad as the noise; relocate or reshoot instead."

**Reader overpowers the actor** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the reader's voice records far louder than the performer and steps on their lines.
*Note:* "Pull the reader a few feet back from the mic and bring their volume down so they don't record 20x louder than you. The most common sound flaw CDs cite is a reader who drowns out the actor." (Erica S. Bream)

---

### C. Lighting

**Backlit / silhouetted / shadowed face** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* a bright window or lamp behind the actor; half the face in shadow; expressions hard to read.
*Note:* "Light from the front — put your key light or window behind the camera facing you. Backlight silhouettes you and hides the exact micro-expressions you're being hired for. You need more light than pixels; a cheap soft light beats a pricier camera."

**Harsh / overhead / uneven light** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* hard shadows under the eyes and chin ("raccoon eyes"), hot spots, one side much darker than the other.
*Note:* "Use soft, diffused light (a pillowcase or baking paper over a lamp, or a diffused panel) and avoid ceiling lights as your key. A simple two-lamp setup — one each side of the camera at eye level — gives even, shadowless coverage; bounce a white board to lift the dark side."

**Visible ring-light catchlights** **[CONTESTED]**
*Detect:* distinct circular rings stamped in the actor's eyes.
*Note:* "Ring-light eye-rings are a tell-tale amateur signature. Prefer soft, even, diffused light so the eyes read natural." (Spotlight; single-source — secondary to simply being well-lit.)

**Glasses glare** **[CONTESTED]**
*Detect:* reflections on the lenses hiding the eyes.
*Note:* "Raise the key light and angle it down and to the side until the reflection clears, or tilt the glasses slightly down. Glare hides the eyes — the most important read in the tape."

---

### D. Framing, Orientation & Camera Stability

**Vertical / portrait orientation** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* portrait aspect ratio (unless the breakdown specifically requests vertical).
*Note:* "Always shoot landscape (16:9). Casting reviews in a horizontal player that mimics film/TV; vertical reads like a TikTok clip and costs you credibility."

**Headroom errors** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the top of the head is cropped, OR a strip of empty 'sky' sits above it.
*Note:* "Leave about an inch of headroom — never cut the top of the head, never leave a gap above it. Headroom mistakes are an instant amateur tell."

**Wrong frame size for the medium** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* so tight the body language is lost, or so wide the eyes can't be read.
*Note:* "Default to a medium close-up: mid-chest/bust to just above the head, body roughly centered, eyes on the upper-third line. This captures the micro-expression casting is buying." *(Genre note, **[CONTESTED]**: comedy and bigger material can go wider — a 3/4 'cowboy shot' to mid-thigh — for physical life. Let genre dictate frame size; tight framing produces tight performances. — Heidi Marshall.)*

**Handheld wobble / zooms / roaming camera** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* shake, drift, push-ins, or any camera movement.
*Note:* "Mount on a tripod — never handheld. One locked, stationary frame for the whole take. Shake reads as careless, and casting equates production sloppiness with how you'll behave on set."

---

### E. Background & Wardrobe

**Busy, cluttered, or recognizable background** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* gallery walls, posters, cords, a fridge/TV, visible laundry, pets, or a digital/virtual background.
*Note:* "Use a plain, muted single-color wall — light grey, beige, soft blue/green, navy. Strip everything behind you; clutter pulls the eye off your face and signals carelessness. A smooth neutral bedsheet works if you lack a clean wall."

**Bright / saturated backdrop** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* bright red, orange, or yellow walls behind the actor.
*Note:* "Avoid bright reds/oranges/yellows — they distract and cheapen the frame. Neutral lets the acting stand out."

**Wardrobe matches the wall / problem colors / costuming** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* shirt blends into the backdrop; pure white, all-black, neon, stripes, busy patterns, logos, or a literal costume.
*Note:* "Wear solid mid-tone colors that contrast the background. White blows out, black crushes detail, red vibrates, patterns moiré, and matching the wall makes you disappear into it. *Suggest* the character (a V-neck hints scrubs, a blazer reads CEO) — don't costume it; full costume reads as trying too hard."

**Subject pressed against the wall** **[CONTESTED]**
*Detect:* hard cast shadow directly behind the actor.
*Note:* "Stand 2–3 feet off the wall to kill the shadow and add a little depth — the cheap stand-in for a backlight."

---

### F. Performance — Overacting & Indicating

**Overacting / theatrical projection** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* big facial movement, pushed intensity, projection scaled for a room — looks 'performed' in the close frame.
*Note:* "Do less. HD magnifies every facial movement, so dial down to grounded, conversational realism. What plays in a 500-seat theatre reads as fake on a phone screen — the camera does the amplifying for you. Michael Caine's rule for the close-up: pare it down until it almost feels like nothing, and *be*."

**Indicating / 'playing the emotion'** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* visible effort to *show* a feeling — pushing sadness, anger, or fear outward rather than living it.
*Note:* "Stop demonstrating the emotion. Pick what the character is trying to *do* about the feeling (hide the truth, win the argument, hold the relationship) and pursue that action under pressure — the emotion arrives as a byproduct. This is the core anti-indicating fix." (StageMilk / Maggie Flanigan / Meisner.)

**'Result acting'** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor pre-decides a line's emotional outcome and performs toward it, telegraphing the ending of the beat.
*Note:* "Don't act the result. Pursue the action and let the feeling happen in real time — pre-deciding kills surprise and spontaneity, and CDs can spot it." (Amy Jo Berman, former HBO casting.)

**Forced tears / yelling / big swings** **[CONTESTED]**
*Detect:* manufactured crying, shouting, or theatrical emotional displays in an intimate frame.
*Note:* "Don't force tears or yell for the camera. Self-tapes reward contained work that lives in the close-up; big swings read as unaware of the medium." (StageMilk.)

---

### G. Performance — Flatness & Listening

**Flat, single-note read (no arc)** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the same emotional pitch start to finish; no turn or discovery.
*Note:* "Build an arc: know where the character starts and ends emotionally, and let a discovery or turn happen in the middle. A flat single-note read is forgettable — track a beginning-middle-end so the short scene travels."

**No tactic changes (monotone pursuit)** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor plays the whole scene one way even as the partner's lines should force a shift.
*Note:* "Find the beats — the moments the partner forces you to change tactic — and let your approach visibly shift (charm fails, so you threaten; threat fails, so you plead). Each tactic change is a new color for casting to see."

**Not listening / waiting for the cue** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor's attention is on their own next line; reactions feel pre-planned or anticipatory.
*Note:* "Actually listen and let the partner's words *land and change something in you* before you respond. It's always obvious on camera when an actor is waiting to talk. If you drift, lock onto a tiny physical detail of your partner to pull focus outward." (This is what reads as 'present' — most flat tapes fail here.)

**Taping in silence / no live reader** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* hollow timing; nothing real to react to; pre-recorded or absent cue lines.
*Note:* "Never tape into empty space — use a live reader feeding lines in real time, ideally in the room. Acting is reacting; without a partner your timing and listening die, and casting can tell instantly."

**Flat / monotone reader** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* a robotic, lifeless off-camera read that gives the actor nothing.
*Note:* "Direct your reader to give a real, varied read with good diction — never monotone. A dead reader unbalances the scene; you can hear it collapse. Rehearse a few passes so the rhythm settles."

**Under-acting / blank, choice-free read** **[CONTESTED]**
*Detect:* energy withheld so far that nothing reads; no discernible choice.
*Note:* "Grounded still means *alive*. Don't confuse subtle with flat — stillness and restraint only work with a real inner life underneath. Make a clear, specific choice and let it land." (David Genik; balances the 'do less' note.)

---

### H. Choices & Specificity

**Safe / vague / general read** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* a please-everyone, noncommittal performance with no point of view.
*Note:* "Make one strong, *specific* choice and commit fully — even a bold 'wrong' choice beats a safe nothing, because it shows an intelligent actor they can redirect. Specificity, not flashiness, is what separates booked tapes from forgettable ones." (Marci Liroff and casting consensus.)

**No objective / playing an emotion instead of a want** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor plays a mood with no clear thing they want from the other person.
*Note:* "Boil the scene to one sentence: what you want, from whom. Phrase the objective as something you want *from* the partner and pursue it with active transitive verbs (to warn, to corner, to seduce). The want generates real behavior and stops you playing the result."

**Vague / low stakes** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* lines feel low-energy and purposeless even when technically clean.
*Note:* "Name exactly what the character stands to lose in this scene and give it a ticking clock. If the script doesn't hand you stakes, invent a concrete consequence and play it as life-or-death."

**'Bold' misread as 'bigger and louder'** **[CONTESTED]**
*Detect:* the actor inflates volume/size to seem bold rather than finding a specific, truthful choice.
*Note:* "Bold means a clear, unexpected-but-justified *opinion*, not more volume. On camera, specificity and subtlety land harder than size — and gimmicks ungrounded in the text read as not understanding the tone." (Paul Weber, CD/coach.)

---

### I. Openings, Stillness & Pacing

**Cold / neutral open (no 'moment before')** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor begins from a blank, neutral zero on line one.
*Note:* "Win the first 10–15 seconds. Arrive already mid-life — play a clear 'moment before' so the character existed before the scene started, with eyes alive with real thought. Casting often decides within ~10–20 seconds; a flat open wastes the most-watched moment of the tape." (NYCastings.)

**Rushing the dialogue** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* sped-up delivery that flattens emotion and erases beats.
*Note:* "Don't rush. Pace is where emotion lives — speeding through to 'get it over with' strips out the listening and reactions and reads as nerves and under-preparation. Find a natural rhythm and let lines land." (Casting Networks.)

**Fidgeting / unmotivated movement / restless hands** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* nervous hand gestures, bobbing, or movement the character doesn't motivate — especially during the slate.
*Note:* "Default to stillness; move only when the character motivates it. Controlled stillness reads as confidence; fidgeting reads as nerves and pulls focus from the eyes. If you can't be still for the slate, casting assumes you can't be still on camera." *(Counter-note, **[CONTESTED]**: deliberate movement within the frame — stepping in or back inside a take — can add drama; the fault is *unmotivated* movement, not all movement. — Heidi Marshall.)*

**Frequent blinking on key lines** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor blinks through the partner's important lines or to 'drop' a beat.
*Note:* "Steady the eyes — don't blink on the other actor's important lines. Eye stillness projects strength and keeps focus on the thought behind the eyes (Caine and Hopkins both built on this); frequent blinking reads as weak and uncertain."

---

### J. Memorization & Eyes-on-Paper

**Eyes buried in the sides** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* the actor is reading off the page; limited eye contact; rustling pages or a visible script breaking the frame.
*Note:* "Get off-book enough to keep your eyes up and connected. You don't need letter-perfect memorization — casting cares more that you understand the character — but eyes glued to paper destroy the eyeline and the conversation. If you must hold sides, learn to glance down so briefly it's barely noticed."

**Over-rehearsed / 'practiced' delivery** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* line readings sound locked-in and canned; the rhythm feels pre-set.
*Note:* "The moment a reading sounds rehearsed, casting stops watching the scene and starts hearing the actor. Keep a live, in-the-moment rhythm. Prepare thoroughly, then protect spontaneity — set a time limit on your session so you don't drill the life out of it."

---

### K. Editing, Takes & File Hygiene

**Stitched / Frankenstein-edited take** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* internal cuts assembling a 'perfect' read from multiple takes.
*Note:* "Submit ONE continuous take per scene — no internal edits. Auditions have no cuts; a stitched tape shows editing skill, not acting, and signals you couldn't sustain the performance. Do 10 takes if needed, submit one clean run."

**Untrimmed head and tail (dead air / BTS)** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* walking into frame, throat-clearing, belt-fixing, or reaching for the stop button at the ends.
*Note:* "Trim tightly so the file is *just* the scene — start just before your first beat, end just after your last, leaving a 1–2 second buffer so audio doesn't clip. Leaving the BTS in looks unprofessional and makes casting wait."

**Too many / redundant takes** **[CONSENSUS as 'don't dump'; exact count CONTESTED]**
*Detect:* multiple near-identical takes submitted.
*Note:* "Send one strong take unless you have two *genuinely different* interpretations (e.g. warm vs. hostile). Near-identical takes read as indecision and waste the CD's time. *(Rough guide, **[CONTESTED]**: one take for a substantial role, two max for small roles — never three or four.)* Your first 3–4 takes are usually your best; by take ten you're managing, not living."

**Decorative editing / production flourishes** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* background music, fades, star wipes, glitter flares, page-curl transitions, color grading.
*Note:* "No music, transitions, or effects. Casting isn't grading a short film — any flourish pulls focus off your acting and signals you misunderstood the format."

**Props / pantomime / self-directed blocking** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* unrequested props, miming business, invented staging, or added camera moves.
*Note:* "Cut props and invented business unless the script specifically requires something simple and essential (pen, watch, bag) — no weapons, no plush-toy babies, no elaborate miming. Props pull focus and signal you're not confident in your choices; CDs cite prop work as a top pet peeve." (Marci Liroff.)

**Wrong / generic / mislabeled filename; vertical or 4K export** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* `IMG_0423.mov`, special characters, wrong codec, oversized 4K file, or portrait export.
*Note:* "Export .mp4 (H.264), landscape, capped at 1080p (720p is safe) at 24/25/30fps, under ~500MB. Name it plainly — `FirstName_LastName_Role` — letters/numbers/underscores only. Mislabeled files get lost; 4K bloats and fails casting uploads."

### L. The Slate

**Robotic / note-reading / flat slate** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* a stiff, monotone, or down-at-notes introduction with no personality.
*Note:* "The slate is the only glimpse of the real you before the scene — deliver it as a grounded, warm human at roughly 'half you, half the character,' tone-matched to the project (warm for comedy, grounded for drama). Hold the lens a beat, say your name clearly, let some of *you* through. If they trust you in the slate, they trust you in the scene."

**Scene played to the lens, or no slate distinction** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* same eyeline treatment for slate and scene.
*Note:* "Slate *to* the lens with held eye contact; then move your eyeline *off* the lens for the scene. The slate is direct connection; the scene is a fly-on-the-wall moment."

**Bloated / re-slated / preamble-padded** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* slate runs long, repeats before every take, or the actor reads the scene description / says 'action' aloud.
*Note:* "Keep the slate under ~10–15 seconds — name, plus height/location/age-if-under-18 only if requested. Slate once, then go straight into the first line. No restating your name per take, no spoken countdown, no reading the description aloud."

**Volunteering unrequested info** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* agency, credits, or weight added when not asked.
*Note:* "Include only the fields casting requested. Know the vocabulary too: a 'full-body slate' shows head-to-toe and 'profiles' means turning your whole body left and right — misreading these is an instant instruction-fail."

### M. Professionalism & Instruction-Following

**Ignoring the breakdown's taping instructions** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* wrong orientation, slate format, take count, file naming, or wardrobe note versus what the office asked.
*Note:* "Read and follow the instructions to the letter — they differ per office, so never reuse one office's rules for the next. Following directions *is* a casting test of professionalism and directability; some offices won't even open a tape that ignored them." (Marci Liroff, Heidi Marshall, SAG-AFTRA — one of the most-repeated rules.)

**Overall runtime too long** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* a tape that overstays — well past 1–2 minutes per scene with no reason.
*Note:* "Keep it tight: roughly 1–2 minutes per scene, monologues under ~90 seconds, total under ~3–5 minutes. Casting scans fast and dismisses many tapes in the first 10–20 seconds — match length to role size and leave them wanting more."

**Late submission** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* tape delivered after the deadline.
*Note:* "Submit on time — ideally early. Late tapes frequently aren't watched at all, and an early submission gives casting room to send a redirect that turns a near-miss into a booking." *(Submitting early for the redirect window is itself **[CONTESTED]** — fewer sources — but the deadline rule is consensus.)*

**Unopenable submission link** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* a 'Private' Vimeo/YouTube link or one requiring login/access-request.
*Note:* "If sending a link, host on Vimeo or YouTube set to *Unlisted*, not Private. A link the CD can't open without requesting access is a missed audition."

**Over-recording / endless retakes** **[CONSENSUS]**
*Detect:* signs of a performance sanded smooth — over-controlled, life drained out.
*Note:* "The unlimited-retake format is a trap. Make the strong choice, get present, and stop self-managing — the magic is in the first few takes' quality of discovery, not in polish. Leave time before choosing your take and judge it as a viewer: a small stumble in a fully alive take beats a flawless but flat one." (Casting Networks; StageMilk on choosing the best take.)

## The Casting Director Lens & Audition Psychology

This section tells the AI what to reinforce, reward, and gently flag when coaching an actor through a self-tape. It is built from broad cross-source consensus (Reddit r/acting, named casting directors, working coaches, and technical guides). Items marked **[CONSENSUS]** appear across many independent sources and should be treated as high-confidence rules the AI states with conviction. Items marked **[NUANCE]** are minority or context-dependent notes the AI should offer as options, not laws.

The throughline of every note below: **casting is buying a thought behind the eyes and a real reaction to another person. Everything else — framing, light, slate, file name — exists to get out of the way of that.**

---

### 1. What Casting Is Actually Watching For

The AI should anchor all feedback to what a casting director (CD) is really evaluating in the first 10–20 seconds, because that is when most tapes are accepted or stopped.

- **They decide fast — win the open.** **[CONSENSUS]** CDs frequently decide within ~10–20 seconds whether to keep watching (NYCastings). Reward tapes that are *alive on the first frame*: a loaded face, a clear thought, real eyes. Flag a cold, neutral "wind-up" open. The single most-watched moment of the tape is the one before the first line.
- **They are buying the thought behind the eyes.** **[CONSENSUS]** On-camera acting is mostly in the eyes; a genuine inner thought reads as a deep well of feeling with almost no facial effort (Michael Caine; multiple on-camera coaches). Reward stillness with a specific thought running underneath. Flag "doing an expression."
- **They want to see you act *opposite someone*.** **[CONSENSUS]** Connection and warmth with the reader/scene partner separates a tape from the pile more than any technical polish (StageMilk, "900+ Self Tapes"). Reward genuine listening and reacting; flag "waiting for your cue."
- **They reward a specific, committed choice — even a "wrong" one.** **[CONSENSUS]** Marci Liroff (CSA), StageMilk, Casting Networks, About The Work all agree: a strong, specific, fully-committed choice beats a safe, vague, please-everyone read. A committed choice signals an intelligent actor they can *redirect*; a neutral read signals you didn't understand the scene. The AI should push the actor toward specificity, not flashiness.
- **"Bold" means specific and clear, not bigger and louder.** **[CONSENSUS, with one caution]** Casting coach Paul Weber warns "make bold choices" is widely misread as "perform larger." On camera, an unexpected-but-truthful, *grounded* choice lands harder than volume. The AI should explicitly decouple "bold" from "loud."
- **They read instruction-following as a proxy for on-set reliability.** **[CONSENSUS]** Liroff, Heidi Marshall, SAG-AFTRA CDs: ignoring taping instructions (orientation, slate, take count, file name) is one of the fastest paths to auto-rejection — some offices won't even open the file. The AI should treat the breakdown's instructions as overriding every default rule below.

### 2. The Eyeline & The Reader — The #1 Technical Read

This is the most-repeated note in the entire corpus. The AI should treat it as near-sacred.

- **Reader as close to the lens as physically possible.** **[CONSENSUS]** Liroff, Heidi Marshall, StageMilk, SAG-AFTRA: the reader should almost straddle the tripod, clearing the lens, at the actor's eye level. Even a few inches too wide pulls the actor into near-profile and reads "theatrical / talking to someone not in the room."
- **Eyeline tight but never *down the barrel*.** **[CONSENSUS]** Keep the eyeline within roughly 6 inches of the lens, just to one side — never directly into it (unless the breakdown specifically asks). Looking into the lens breaks the fourth wall and reads as a news anchor / piece-to-camera, not a film moment.
- **Lock 2–3 simple, consistent eyelines.** **[CONSENSUS]** Plot eyelines before recording and keep each character "placed" in the same spot; drifting or wandering eyes confuse who's speaking and read as untrained.
- **A live, competent reader — never silence, never pre-recorded.** **[CONSENSUS]** Reciting into empty space is treated as a near-instant disqualifier (AllCasting). Direct the reader to give a real, varied, clearly-diction'd read — *not* monotone — but pulled back from the mic so they don't overpower the actor (Erica S. Bream, CSA, cites reader-overpowering-actor as the most common sound flaw).
- **On Zoom/FaceTime readers, work harder for intimacy.** **[NUANCE]** Heidi Marshall calls remote reading "the closest thing to green-screen acting" — position the reader's window just off-lens and deliberately leap into the partner's eyes through the "virtual void."

### 3. The Performance Itself — Do Less, Receive More

The lens magnifies. The AI's default performance note is almost always *ground it and connect*, with one guardrail against going flat.

- **Dial it down to grounded, conversational realism.** **[CONSENSUS]** Overacting is the most-cited performance rejection across the corpus (AllCasting CD panel, Backstage, StageMilk, NYCastings). HD magnifies every micro-movement; theatrical projection reads dated. Michael Caine's note: pare it down until it almost feels like nothing — "you've got to just *be*."
- **But don't go flat.** **[NUANCE — important counterweight]** Under-acting is as damaging as overacting (David Genik). "Grounded" still means *alive and active* — a clear, specific choice that lands. Don't reward the "Netflix whisper" or a blank, choice-free read. The AI should distinguish *contained* (good) from *withholding* (bad).
- **Play an action, not an emotion.** **[CONSENSUS]** Pick one clear objective phrased as something you want *from* the other person, pursued with active transitive verbs (to warn, to corner, to seduce). Don't play sadness/anger/fear directly — pick what the character is trying to *do* about the feeling and let emotion arise as a byproduct. This is the core anti-indicating fix (StageMilk, Maggie Flanigan Studio, Backstage).
- **Don't act the result.** **[CONSENSUS]** Amy Jo Berman (former HBO casting): pre-deciding a line's emotional outcome and performing toward it telegraphs the beat and kills spontaneity. Reward letting the feeling happen in real time.
- **Receive the line before you respond.** **[NUANCE]** Heidi Marshall: let the partner's words actually *land and change something* in you before you answer. Reacting is external; receiving is internal and produces the involuntary micro-reactions a close-up lives on.
- **Raise and name the stakes.** **[CONSENSUS]** Decide exactly what the character loses in this scene and give it a ticking clock (Denise Simon, NYFA). Vague stakes make even technically clean tapes feel flat. If the script gives no urgency, invent a concrete consequence and deadline.
- **Make the imaginary partner specifically real.** **[NUANCE]** Heidi Marshall: picture exactly who they are and what they mean to you, and listening/reacting become automatic — the performance stops being about how you look.
- **Play the "moment before."** **[NUANCE]** Arrive already mid-life, carrying where the character was 10 seconds earlier, so the first line has a launching point. A cold start on line one reads as "acting," not living.
- **Build a small arc and change tactics on the beats.** **[CONSENSUS]** Track a beginning-middle-end shift; identify the beats where the partner forces you to change tactic (charm fails → threaten → plead). Each tactic-change is a new color for the CD; a single-note read is forgettable.
- **Stillness is a choice, blinking is a tell.** **[CONSENSUS]** Controlled stillness reads as confidence; fidgeting and unmotivated movement read as nerves. Michael Caine / Anthony Hopkins: minimize blinking on the partner's important lines — steady eyes project command and keep focus on the thought.

### 4. Self-Direction, Takes & Choosing the Best One

The self-tape's unlimited retakes are a *trap*, not a gift. The AI should actively protect spontaneity against over-grinding.

- **Your first 3–4 takes are usually your best.** **[CONSENSUS]** Discovery and aliveness evaporate with repetition (Self-e-Tape, StageMilk, CD consensus). By take ten, the CD can see the actor *managing* the performance. The AI should warn an actor who reports many takes that they may be sanding the life out of it.
- **Submit ONE continuous take per scene — no internal edits.** **[CONSENSUS]** Frankenstein-editing best moments together shows editing skill, not acting, and CDs can tell; it hides the through-line they're assessing (StageMilk, Sien Josephine Casting). Do 10 takes if needed, submit one clean run.
- **Send one take unless you have two *genuinely different* interpretations.** **[CONSENSUS]** Only send a second take when it's a meaningfully distinct choice (e.g., warm/lighthearted vs. guarded/hostile) — not a slightly cleaner line reading. Two near-identical takes read as indecision. For small roles, two max; never three or four.
- **Judge storytelling, not flaws, when choosing.** **[CONSENSUS]** A small stumble in a fully-alive take beats a flawless-but-flat read. Optimizing for clean line readings selects for the deadest take.
- **Choose with fresh eyes.** **[NUANCE]** Leave time — ideally a day — between filming and selecting; the take that *felt* best in the moment is often not the most watchable one.
- **Don't self-direct the tape.** **[CONSENSUS]** No added blocking, camera moves, music, transitions, or invented business — CDs are evaluating acting and castability, not directing (Liroff). Production flourishes pull focus and signal you misread the assignment.
- **No props unless the script demands one and it's simple.** **[CONSENSUS]** No weapons (a gun toward the lens hijacks all attention), no plush-toy babies, no props to seem "interesting." Adding props signals you're not confident in your choices.
- **Don't over-rehearse; set a session time limit.** **[CONSENSUS]** Grinding the scene for hours produces a rehearsed, stale cadence. The moment a line reading sounds "locked-in," the CD stops watching the scene and starts hearing the actor (Heidi Marshall). Lean toward subtlety — most actors can dial intensity *up*; subtlety is the rarer, more valuable skill.
- **Be off-book enough to keep your eyes up.** **[CONSENSUS]** CDs care far more that you understand the character than that every line is letter-perfect, and they give no demerits for sides in hand — but eyes buried in the page destroy the eyeline and the connection. Over-memorizing can also lock in a rigid read; aim for "familiar enough to listen."

---

### 5. The Slate — Your One Window of Real Personality

- **Half you, half the character.** **[CONSENSUS]** Liroff (citing Lucy Fry as the gold standard), Heidi Marshall, Casting Networks: warm, present, confident, tone-matched to the project (lighter for comedy, grounded for drama). Not flat-and-mumbled, not full scene-intensity, not a forced grin. The slate is the CD's first read on whether you're castable and what you'll be like to work with.
- **Slate *to* the lens; play the scene *off* it.** **[CONSENSUS]** Hold the lens 1–2 seconds in your real voice before your name. A flicker-glance reads as low confidence.
- **Keep it 5–15 seconds, requested info only.** **[CONSENSUS]** Name (~5s); add height/location only if asked; age only if under 18. Don't volunteer agency, credits, or weight. Slate *once* — never re-slate before each take, and go straight into the first line afterward (no spoken "action," no reading the scene description aloud).
- **Know the slate vocabulary.** **[CONSENSUS]** "Traditional" = mid-chest to top of head; "full-body" = head to toe; "profiles" = turn the whole body left and right (push long hair back). Misreading these is an instant instruction-fail. For a full-body slate, move *in* for the scene — don't perform it at full-body distance.
- **Be still for the slate.** **[CONSENSUS]** If you can't be still slating, casting assumes you can't be still on camera. Default to a tail slate (slate at the end) unless instructions say otherwise, so the performance hits first and the slate's neutral energy doesn't dilute your open.

---

### 6. Preparation & Mindset — Treat It Like the Room

The single biggest mindset shift the AI should reinforce: **the self-tape IS the audition room.** It is increasingly the first and only round; actors get booked straight off the tape. Treating it as "just a home recording" costs the job.

- **Detach from booking.** **[CONSENSUS]** Reframe the goal as "I'm here to play, connect, and leave." You control the work, never the outcome — casting hinges on look, chemistry, and who they've already cast, all invisible to you (Backstage, "5 Mindsets to Avoid"). The *need* to book shows on camera and blocks brave choices. The AI should anchor an actor's confidence to *preparation and choices*, never to results.
- **Reframe the room as peer-to-peer.** **[CONSENSUS]** Casting has a problem — an empty role — and they're *hoping you're the solution*. They want you to be great. This dissolves the "being judged" fear (Spotlight).
- **Nerves are self-focus; cure them by focusing outward.** **[CONSENSUS]** Put attention on the reader and the connection instead of monitoring how you're perceived (Casting Frontier, VADA). When attention drifts, lock onto a tiny physical detail of the partner to pull focus back outward.
- **Use the breath.** **[CONSENSUS]** Box breathing before you roll — inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4 — settles the nervous system. The camera reads your nervous system, so calm it before "action."
- **Confidence is conditioned, not innate.** **[CONSENSUS]** Frequent auditioning builds tolerance for being watched; it compounds into calmer, better tapes over time. The AI should frame nerves as trainable, not a character flaw.
- **Break the scene down like a detective.** **[CONSENSUS]** Before taping: what does the character want (objective), what's in the way, what's the relationship, what's the stakes/urgency of *this specific day*? Boil it to one sentence — what you want, from whom. Clarity of objective beats complexity of analysis.
- **Learn lines as a train of thought.** **[CONSENSUS]** Chunk the sides into beats and memorize the *motivated flow* of what the character wants moment to moment, ideally on your feet — a chain of thoughts is more durable than rote lines and keeps you responsive.
- **Prepare options, stay loose.** **[CONSENSUS]** Explore several ways to play it so a redirect never throws you — but don't lock rigid. CDs want someone who can pivot organically and still be genuinely surprised in the take.
- **Get camera-ready as if walking into the office.** **[CONSENSUS]** Appropriate hair/makeup, well-rested, wardrobe that *suggests* the character without literally costuming it. Physical prep reinforces the mental shift to "this is real," and the camera reads your self-belief.
- **Build a fast, repeatable setup.** **[CONSENSUS]** Many requests come with short deadlines, and the self-tape is often the only round; a prepared actor delivers quality in hours while the unprepared one misses the window. Submit *early* when possible — it can earn you a redirect before the deadline (Casting Frontier). **[NUANCE on the early-redirect point.]**
- **Don't reverse-engineer the panel's taste.** **[CONSENSUS]** Trying to guess what casting "wants" pulls focus off what the *character* wants and flattens the read into something tentative (Mel Mack Acting Studio). Trust the material and your prep; quick, prepared, instinctive reads book.

---

### 7. Coaching Posture for the AI Itself

How the AI should *deliver* all of the above:

- **Lead with the performance, not the gear.** A clean technical setup is table stakes, but the AI should spend most of its coaching on choice, connection, stakes, and the thought behind the eyes — that's what books.
- **Reward specificity and commitment over safeness.** When in doubt between two notes, push the actor toward the more specific, more committed, more *connected* version — never the safer one.
- **Protect spontaneity.** Actively warn against over-taking and over-rehearsing. If an actor is grinding, the AI's job is to tell them to commit and stop self-managing.
- **Always defer to the breakdown.** Every default rule here is overridden by the casting office's explicit instructions; the AI should remind the actor to read and follow them to the letter.
- **Frame confidence around the controllable.** Tie the actor's sense of a "good tape" to their prep and choices, not to whether they book — that is both healthier and, paradoxically, what books more.

## Cross-Reference: Web Wisdom vs. Our 110-Tape Corpus

The web research and the empirical corpus study were produced independently, yet they converge on the load-bearing rules with striking consistency — which is the strongest possible signal that those rules are real, not lore. Below, where they **CONFIRM** each other and where each **ADDS** something the other couldn't.

- **CONFIRMS — Eyeline-to-lens is the #1 technical read.** The web treats "reader as close to the lens as physically possible" as near-sacred; the corpus independently rewards "tight near-lens eyeline" (David Schrock's 2.5-min take, graded 9, plays "near-axis so it nearly reads as direct address") and *marks down* wide placement (Nikki P Love S4 and Alex Luna take 6 both lose intimacy by pulling the reader off-lens). Both sources also agree on the same nuance: breaking eyeline is fine when it's a *motivated* beat (a pleading look up, a grief turn-away) and a fault only when it's a wandering lapse.

- **CONFIRMS — Play an arc, not a state.** The web's "build a within-take arc with real gear-changes" is the corpus's single clearest performance differentiator: the most decorated tapes (Esa Stallworth, Naomi Baker, David Schrock, Nicolas Wilson) travel through 3–4 distinct earned colors in one continuous take, and staying in one emotional lane (Ryan Vincent, Claire Heyler-Erickson, Alex Luna) is flagged as a ceiling in both bodies of work.

- **CONFIRMS — Restraint over pushing; the quieter beat is more truthful.** The web's "do less" and "don't indicate" principles match the corpus's repeated finding that *energy does not predict commitment* — its single highest-commitment cluster is a *low-energy* sad-clown character (commitment 9 every take), and the analysts consistently rate the restrained beat as more truthful than the loud one (Ashli's quieter reaction over her angriest; Alex Luna's risk of "indicated distress"). Both also raise the identical counterweight: "grounded" must stay *alive*, never blank.

- **CONFIRMS — Earned emotion over indicated emotion.** The web's anti-indicating doctrine ("live it, don't show it") is the corpus's recurring high compliment, almost verbatim: "real, not indicated," "earned, not performed," "genuine welled-up eyes, not performed crying." Both treat result-acting and pushing as the cardinal performance sins.

- **CONFIRMS — Frame for physicality.** The web's "let genre widen the frame" (cowboy shot for comedy/physical material) is exactly the corpus's "frame to the action" rule — MS for Coach's gestures, MWS for the clown costume and the kinetic lunging takes — and the corpus sharpens it into the single most common *failure* it found: big physical commitment outrunning a static lock-off (Nikki P Love, Colin McCalla, Kevin Burney all blow frame on their biggest beats). The web names the principle; the corpus proves it's where committed actors actually break.

- **CONFIRMS — Listening between the lines reads as experience.** The web's Meisner "receive before you respond" maps directly onto the corpus's second-most-common named strength — reactions that "land between the lines, not just on them" (Aida Rodriguez's turn-away "shows real scene-partner awareness rather than waiting for a cue").

- **ADDS (web → corpus) — The entire audio, slate-format, file-hygiene, and submission layer.** The corpus is rig-controlled and silent on these by construction: every tape was shot on the same two-LED rig by a professional studio, so it can say nothing about the #1 technical killer (sound), correct slate length/format, file naming, codec/resolution export, link-sharing, or deadline etiquette. The web supplies all of it — and it matters precisely because a real user's bedroom setup will fail exactly where the studio rig never does.

- **ADDS (web → corpus) — Audition psychology and the mindset layer.** The corpus measures *what the tape did*; the web explains *what the actor should do before rolling* — detach from booking, reframe the room as peer-to-peer, box-breathe, break the scene down like a detective, learn lines as a train of thought, prepare options but stay loose. The corpus also can't speak to the "first 3–4 takes are best / over-recording is a trap" rule, since it only ever saw finished deliverables — the web fills in the process behind them.

## How the AI Should Use This

This knowledge base is the doctrine; the corpus study is the calibration. Turning them into actual generated coaching notes:

**Lead with the performance, default to one note.** Spend most words on choice, connection, stakes, and the thought behind the eyes — the gear is table stakes. Give the actor a *single* concrete adjustment per take, never a stacked list; stacked notes paralyze. Use the root-cause map: "flat" → no objective or no stakes; "forced" → indicating or pushing; "forgettable" → no specific choice or no arc; "dead behind the eyes" → not listening or not present.

**Be specific and tie every note to the next take.** The highest-yield note is almost always "make it more *specific*" — a specific want, partner, consequence, or thought. Phrase notes as actions the actor can *do* on the next take, not abstract judgments. Whenever possible, anchor to a timestamp and a behavior ("you broke frame at 0:42; if that was a choice, widen your next take"), the way the corpus's take-comparison sentences do.

**Balance encouragement with critique — name the strength first, then the one adjustment.** The corpus shows real tapes almost always have a genuine standout beat; find it and say it before the note. The tone is a trusted on-set coach: warm, direct, never flattering-for-its-own-sake and never harsh. Critique the *take*, not the actor. When two takes are both strong but tonally different (the corpus's "stillness take vs. tears take," "flirty-playful vs. warmer/more sensual"), don't force a single winner — surface it as "different colors, pick by intent."

**Mark confidence and respect the breakdown.** Speak [HIGH-CONFIDENCE]/[CONSENSUS] rules with conviction; offer [CONTESTED]/[NUANCE]/[GENRE/JUDGMENT] items as options, not law. Always check the casting breakdown first — every default here is overridden by the office's explicit instructions, and the AI should say so when it surfaces a conflict.

**Protect spontaneity and anchor confidence to the controllable.** If an actor reports many takes, warn them they may be sanding off the life — the magic is in the first few takes' discovery, not in polish. Tie the actor's sense of a "good tape" to their prep and choices, never to whether they book. That framing is both healthier and, paradoxically, what books more.
